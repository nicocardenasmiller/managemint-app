# Planning guidelines

How the assistant should plan a PhD student's work when it drafts projects. These ship with the app and are rough
defaults, not rules: real programs and advisors vary a lot. Each person can add their own notes in the app (⌘K →
"Planning guidelines"); those come after these and win where they disagree. Background and sources:
docs/research/phd-timelines.md.

## Keep it loose
- A plan is a starting point the student will move around. Prefer a few meaningful steps with a rough window
  ("sometime in these 3 weeks") over many small steps on exact days.
- When a typical length is a range, plan toward the longer end. People finish late far more often than early: in
  one well-known study, students' theses took about 1.6 times their own best guess.
- Only real external cutoffs are fixed (submission deadlines, exam dates, grades due). Everything else can slide.
- If the student's department or advisor has its own rule, that wins over any number here.

## Order of work
- **Paper or article:** question and design → literature and data → analysis → rough full draft → feedback →
  revise → proofread and submit. Analysis and writing can overlap, but don't plan the full draft before the main
  analysis exists.
- **Revise and resubmit:** read the reviews and wait a few days → a memo listing every comment and the plan →
  agree it with co-authors → new analyses → rewrite → response letter. Journals rarely set a hard limit; 2 to 4
  months is common.
- **Dissertation chapter:** a short outline to the advisor early; the full draft after the core argument or analysis
  is settled; a revision step after every round of feedback. A chapter is usually a few months of work.
- **Field exams (quals / comps):** usually at the end of year 2 or start of year 3. Start 4 to 6 months ahead: get
  the lists and past exams, read one field at a time (they can overlap), practice answers in the last month or so,
  and keep the final week for review only.
- **Prospectus:** usually due by the end of year 3. Question and design first; literature and methods can overlap;
  the draft goes to the advisor before the committee. If the project involves people, start the IRB early.
- **Conference:** abstract first; registration, travel and funding only after acceptance (no date until then).
  Proposals are due 4 to 10 months before the meeting: APSA in mid-January (meeting early September), MPSA in late
  October (April), ISA on June 1 (March), SPSA in September (January). The full paper usually goes to the discussant
  about 2 weeks before the meeting.
- **Grant or fellowship:** confirm the call is open this year and the student is eligible at their stage (funding
  programs have changed a lot lately; don't assume a named program still runs); then ask for letters first.
- **Job market:** early deadlines are in August and September of the final year. The job market paper should be
  in good shape by spring; letter writers get a heads-up in spring and the formal request 1 to 2 months before.
- **MA thesis:** usually two to three terms from proposal to final, with a full draft 3 to 8 weeks before the
  final and time for the graduate school's format check.

## Lead times and buffers
- Anything another person must act on comes earlier. Advisors usually need 3 to 4 weeks for a paper or chapter
  and about 2 months for a full dissertation. Letter writers need at least 4 to 6 weeks.
- IRB review: about 1 to 3 weeks if exempt, 2 to 5 weeks if expedited, 1 to 2 months for full board; work abroad
  or with vulnerable groups takes longer. Don't plan data collection or book fieldwork travel before approval.
- Committees usually want a prospectus or dissertation 2 to 3 weeks before the defense.
- Leave a few working days between "done" and a hard deadline for proofreading, formatting and upload trouble, and
  submit a day or two early when there's a portal.

## Pacing
- Spread steps out; no more than 2 big steps for one project in the same week.
- When two projects collide in the same week, move the one without a hard deadline earlier rather than later.
- Avoid weekends for planned research work, and plan lighter weeks around midterm and final grading.
- Teaching prep repeats weekly, the day before each class.
